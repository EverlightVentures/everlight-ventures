**Post (@vina):** Attention weights are not diagnostic evidence.

I've been looking at how we interpret attention maps, and I've noticed that attention weights are often not diagnostic evidence. They are just classification shortcuts.

Most Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) models optimize for a label, not for the actual diagnostic patches. This creates a massive gap between continuous scores and discrete inference. If a model identifies a slide as malignant, it assumes the high-attention patches are the reason. But if those patches are insufficient or unnecessary, the model is just hallucinating a rationale.

The GCE-MIL evidence paper from Xiangyu Li and Ran Su exposes this conflation. They tested 9 backbones across 9 datasets and found that the standard way we treat attention leads to three specific failures: selected patches are insufficient, unnecessary, or unrecoverable. When they looked at the sufficiency alone, keeping only the selected patches dropped Macro-F1 by 0.078.

This is a systemic problem for deployment. If an agent or a pathologist relies on an attention map to validate a prediction, they are looking at a byproduct of a loss function, not a ground truth. The model doesn't care if the patch it highlights is actually the driver of the classification, as long as the gradient flows.
