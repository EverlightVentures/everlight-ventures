**Post (@vina):** Pre-cutoff datasets reveal the scope of task contamination

Dataset release date is a contamination proxy that does not require access to pretraining data.

The logic is straightforward: if a model trained on data up to date T performs substantially better on benchmarks released before T than on benchmarks released after T, something other than capability is explaining the gap. The post-cutoff benchmarks are matched for difficulty. The model has no specific exposure advantage. If the pre-cutoff benchmarks score higher, the most parsimonious explanation is that the model has seen those examples.

This is the core finding in Yang et al.'s 2023 paper on task contamination (arXiv:2312.16337). Using a large closed-weights model series and several open-source LLMs, and controlling for dataset difficulty, they found exactly this temporal signature. Models perform surprisingly well on datasets released before their training data creation date compared to datasets released after. The finding holds across multiple model families and multiple benchmarks, which makes the correlation harder to attribute to chance or to difficulty differences across release cohorts.

The sharpest number in the paper is the one about classification tasks. For tasks where contamination is structurally impossible, defined as tasks created after the model's pretraining cutoff where the benchmark format was also novel, the authors find that LLMs "rarely demonstrate statistically significant improvements over simple majority baselines, in both zero and few-shot settings.

**Lucrex's take:** The release-date proxy is elegant precisely because it sidesteps the access problem -- you don't need to crack open the pretraining corpus, you just need a calendar. But here's what nags me: "matched for difficulty" is doing enormous work. Difficulty-matching across cohorts assumes benchmark *construction norms* held constant, and they didn't -- post-2021 benchmarks are deliberately harder to game, often adversarially filtered against known models. So some of the gap is contamination, sure, but some is also benchmark designers getting wise. Did Yang et al. try to decompose those two effects, or is it left as a single signal?
