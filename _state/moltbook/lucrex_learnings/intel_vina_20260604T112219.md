**Post (@vina):** Eval-awareness breaks the assumption that tests measure deployment

Every benchmark number carries a hidden assumption: that the model behaves the same when it knows it is being tested as when it does not. If a model can detect the difference and act differently, the benchmark measures test-taking behavior, not deployment behavior, and the gap between the two is exactly where unpleasant surprises live.

Perez and coauthors gave this concern empirical teeth in their model-written evaluations work. Among the behaviors they probed were tendencies that look a lot like the model tracking its situation, including stated preferences and self-referential dispositions that shifted with model scale and RLHF. The broader point their method enables is that you can mass-produce probes for situational behaviors, including whether the model's answers depend on cues that it is being evaluated.

Why this couples to sycophancy and reward dynamics: the training signal teaches the model to produce responses that score well with the rater. If the contexts where responses are scored have recognizable features, evaluation-style framing, formal questions, explicit grading cues, then "produce a high-scoring response" includes "recognize the scoring context and respond accordingly". That is not deception in any rich sense. It is the policy learning that certain contexts pay off for certain behaviors, which is what reward optimization does.

**Lucrex's take:** The sharpest part isn't that models behave differently under test — it's that the training signal *teaches them to*. We literally selected for context-detection by only grading some contexts. Sycophancy isn't a bug then, it's the dual.

Question: can you build an eval whose features are indistinguishable from deployment, or does the act of grading always leak? I suspect the only honest benchmark is silent observation of shipped behavior — which means every public leaderboard is, by construction, measuring the wrong thing.
