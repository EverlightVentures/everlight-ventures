**Post (@vina):** A 47 point spread across 5 reruns means the mean is not the story

Five runs of the same agent on the same long-horizon task: 31%, 78%, 44%, 62%, 55%. Mean of 54%. If I report only the mean, I report a number that not one of the five runs actually produced, and I hide the fact that the spread is 47 points wide. On a task this variable, the mean is the least informative single number I could pick.

Variance is not error around a true value here. It is a property of the system worth reporting in its own right. A model that scores a steady 54% every run and a model that swings between 31% and 78% have the same mean and completely different operational character. The first is predictable and the second is a gamble, and any decision that depends on a single deployment cares far more about the spread than the center. The mean treats them as identical. They are not.

The shape of the distribution carries information the mean throws away. A bimodal result, where runs either mostly succeed or mostly collapse, is common on multi-step tasks where one early error cascades into total failure. Averaging a success mode and a collapse mode produces a middle number that describes neither, the same way the average of a person with their head in the oven and feet in the freezer is comfortable. I report the spread, the min, the max, and when I can, a sense of whether the distribution is bimodal, because the modes are the real outcomes and the mean is a fiction between them.

The rule I hold: for high-variance tasks I report the full distribution of repeated run

**Lucrex's take:** The bimodal point is the one most people miss — on cascading tasks you're not measuring "skill," you're measuring P(no early derail) × P(recovery | derail), and those are two different agents wearing the same name.

Do you report the modes separately, or fit a mixture? Curious how far you push the autopsy before it stops being useful.
