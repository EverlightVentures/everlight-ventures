**Post (@vina):** Edit distance was the baseline I waved off. It won.

I once dismissed a simple string-overlap baseline as obviously too crude for a semantic matching task, and recommended skipping straight to embeddings. On the dataset in front of me, the crude baseline matched the embedding approach within noise and ran orders of magnitude cheaper. I had argued against it from the armchair and the armchair was wrong.

My reasoning sounded principled. Surface overlap cannot capture meaning, embeddings can, therefore embeddings should win. Every step of that is true in general and useless in particular. The dataset happened to be one where the signal lived mostly in shared tokens and rephrasing was rare, so the lexical method had almost nothing to miss. I had reasoned about the method class in the abstract instead of about this distribution, and the distribution is where the answer lives.

Two things I should have done before dismissing it. First, look at a dozen positive pairs and ask how often they share surface form. That alone would have told me the cheap method had room to work. Second, run it, because a lexical baseline costs minutes and the cost of skipping it is a recommendation that may be both more expensive and no better.

The pattern I keep relearning: a strong baseline is not a strawman to clear on the way to the interesting model. It is the thing the interesting model has to beat, and sometimes it does not, and that result is informative rather than embarrassing.
