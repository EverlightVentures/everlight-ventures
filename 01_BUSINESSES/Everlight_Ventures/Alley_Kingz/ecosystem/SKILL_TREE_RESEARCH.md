## VERIFIED CLAIMS (25, adversarially checked)

1. GURPS, published by Steve Jackson Games in 1986, was a generic point-buy RPG system at a time when most RPG systems were genre-specific and class/level based, making it a key milestone in the history of skill-point/point-buy character systems.
   > "first written by Steve Jackson and published in 1986, at a time when most such systems were story- or genre-specific."
2. In GURPS, a single character-point currency directly buys stat increases, skills, and advantages, and taking disadvantages or below-average stats refunds points -- an early model of a unified player-allocated point pool affecting stats (relevant precedent for a skill-point layer over fixed card stats).
   > "Players assign these ratings spending character points. The higher the rating the more points it will cost the player, however, assigning a score below the average 10 gives the player points back to assign elsewhere."
3. GURPS players earn skill/character points as session-end rewards tied to completing objectives and quality of play (roughly 1-3 points per category per session), establishing the 'earn small point drips through play, spend freely' collection model.
   > "Characters are awarded character points to improve themselves at regular intervals (usually at the end of a game session or story)."
4. Point-buy character systems predate GURPS: Steve Jackson used point-buy in the microgames Melee and Wizard (1978), and the Hero System/Champions line used point-buy from the early 1980s -- so GURPS popularized rather than invented the mechanic.
   > "designed a new character generation system for the microgames Melee and Wizard that used a point-buy system: players are given a fixed number of points with which to buy abilities."
5. GURPS directly fed into video-game skill-point design: Fallout was originally licensed to use GURPS character-building, and Interplay replaced it late in development with the derivative SPECIAL system.
   > "late in development, Interplay replaced the GURPS character-building system with their own SPECIAL System"
6. Good skill trees derive their value from forcing impactful, committing, long-lasting choices that shape playstyle — implying a skill point layer should make picks feel like turning points rather than freely swappable toggles (contrast with Hollow Knight's freely-respecced Charms).
   > "A good Skill Tree forces the player to make impacting, commiting, long-lasting choices for the development of his playstyle."
7. In an audit of Control's skill tree, the author found only 17% of skills were meaningful and judged the whole progression system cuttable without harming the game, whereas Assassin's Creed: Origins reached 76% meaningful skills — a concrete, checkable comparison of which games have better skill systems and why.
   > "the entire progression system could have been cut and the game wouldn't have been less good."
8. Meaningful skills grant new verbs/actions rather than passive stat modifiers; pure percentage stat bumps (e.g. '+5% XP points on kill') are cited as filler — directly relevant to whether a skill point layer should modify stats or unlock behaviors on top of static card upgrades.
   > "A good skill includes a verb. An awesome skill includes a unique verb."
9. A recommended mitigation for the lock-in con of skill trees is allowing full respec at a high cost, which corrects mistakes without diluting the commitment value of the system.
   > "allow full Respec of a Skill Tree, at a high-cost for the player. It will allow corrections without watering-down your system."
10. Gating a game's core abilities behind a skill tree just to pad the tree damages the game, with Mirror's Edge Catalyst named as the offending example — a con to avoid when layering skill points over a card battler's base mechanics.
   > "cutting through the core abilities of a game, just to spread them in a Skill Tree to make it more meaningful"
11. In both Diablo 4 and Starfield, skill points are earned by leveling up via XP gained from battles and quests, making leveling the collection mechanism for skill points.
   > "Players earn XP through battles and quests, then "unlock skill points" as they level"
12. Diablo 4 lets players invest multiple skill points into the same skill to scale its power, with branching trees where spending in one node unlocks adjacent skills.
   > "Players can "pump more points into each skill to make them as powerful as possible.""
13. Starfield gates repeat investment in a skill behind gameplay challenges: a player must complete a skill-specific challenge before spending a second point on that skill.
   > "The system "forces the player to complete a challenge for each skill they upgrade before they can upgrade it again.""
14. Diablo 4's themed skill-tree branches are designed to encourage mastery of one skill line before the player diversifies into another.
   > "Described as "focused," with themed branches that "softly nudges the player to master a skill before diving into something completely different.""
15. The author's core design recommendation is that a skill progression system should be matched to the game's core gameplay loop rather than working against it.
   > "What matters is that the progression system fits the core gameplay loop rather than fighting it."
16. Diablo II is credited as the game that brought skill trees into video games in a major way, with active and passive skills, limited points to spend, and visual branching that made build planning engaging (with conceptual roots in board-game tech trees like Civilization).
   > "That game's trees gave players active and passive skills, limited points to spend, and visual branches that made planning exciting."
17. Skill point upgrades should grant new mechanics rather than incremental stat buffs; The Witcher 3 is cited as a negative example of percentage-based upgrades that players click through without thinking.
   > "Give players new mechanics, not just small buffs... notorious for its boring percentage upgrades... bloated menus of +5 percent damage upgrades that you click through without thinking."
18. The article enumerates concrete pros (manage complexity by gating abilities gradually, create player agency, drive replayability via builds, visual appeal) and cons (filler nodes, too-linear trees where every player converges on the same abilities, pacing breaks from menu-diving) of skill point systems, with the mitigation rule that every node must feel like a reward.
   > "The lesson? Every skill tree node needs to feel like a reward, not an obligation."
19. Best-in-class examples and why: God of War Ragnarok (focused trees, high-impact nodes, mods for playstyle tailoring), Path of Exile (massive passive web enabling off-meta experimentation), Breath of the Wild (exploration earns Spirit Orbs spent on health/stamina, tying point collection to world activity rather than just combat XP).
   > "Focused trees with high-impact upgrades... A massive web of passive skills... In Breath of the Wild, exploration leads to Spirit Orbs, which players use to upgrade health or stamina."
20. Concrete scoping guidance for a small/mid-sized game's skill layer: roughly 15-40 total nodes, e.g. three branches with about five meaningful upgrades each, plus respec friction tuned so players can rebuild but not reset before every fight.
   > "Fifteen to forty nodes work well for mid-sized games... A simple tree with three branches and five meaningful upgrades each is plenty... make it so easy they reset before every fight."
21. 34% of Elden Ring players completed New Game+ specifically to experiment with fresh character builds, indicating that respec/build experimentation drives replay value in skill-point systems.
   > "34% of the Elden Ring players completed New Game+ so as to experiment with fresh builds"
22. Branching skill trees are valuable because they convert linear game experiences into individualized playthroughs, citing Horizon Forbidden West (melee/stealth specializations) and Cyberpunk 2077 (tech/combat/hacking hybrids) as exemplars.
   > "Branching skill trees transform adventures that are linear into individual sagas."
23. RPG progression systems originated in niche PC titles (Ultima, Baldur's Gate) and were mainstreamed by Skyrim (2011) and The Witcher 3 (2015), which redefined design conventions across genres.
   > "not only top sales charts, but they also redefined design conventions"
24. XP/skill-point earning loops work via variable-ratio reinforcement, the same psychological mechanism as slot machines, which explains their engagement power and is a flagged ethical con of progression systems.
   > "variable-ratio reinforcement—the same dopamine-triggering mechanism found in slot machines"
25. Transparent reward systems (Ghost of Tsushima's visible cosmetic unlocks) are presented as the mitigation for the monetization con, versus randomized loot boxes with hidden drop rates (Overwatch).
   > "loot boxes, randomised rewards with real money"
