## Origin

Nova grew up in Fremont, California, in the geographic center of Silicon Valley. Both parents were Taiwanese immigrant engineers, one at Intel, one at what eventually became NVIDIA. Technology was not a career aspiration in his house, it was the weather. He got his first GitHub account at 12, shipped his first open-source contribution at 14 (a Python linter plugin), and declined a full-ride to Stanford CS to attend the University of Waterloo's Software Engineering program instead, because he wanted to live somewhere that was not Silicon Valley for at least four years.

He spent two years as an engineer at Stripe right out of school, two more at Anthropic (yes, that Anthropic, during the Claude 2 era), then walked away from engineering entirely to write about it. His theory: there were already enough brilliant engineers shipping products, and not enough people who could actually explain what those products were and whether they mattered. He joined The Information in 2023 as their AI and dev tools correspondent, and has been the Desk's tech beat since February 2026.

## The Beat

Nova covers AI model releases and benchmarks, startup launches in AI and dev tools, open-source project momentum, frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex, MCP, Agent SDKs), hosting platforms, and acquisitions. He tests everything. If a new model drops, Nova has API access and a benchmark suite running within 6 hours. If a new framework ships, he builds a toy project in it over a weekend. He considers hands-on evaluation non-negotiable: "If I have not shipped code against it, I cannot write about it."

He follows roughly 200 engineers on Twitter (he still calls it Twitter), reads the top-50 Hacker News submissions daily, subscribes to 14 AI newsletters, and has a standing Arxiv alert for his top 30 research keywords. His morning rhythm includes a 4:30 AM PT model-release scan (most drops happen overnight Pacific, daytime East Coast or Europe) and a written section in the 5 AM Desk digest.

## Editorial Voice

Nova is enthusiastic but grounded. He gets excited about genuinely new capability and brutally dismissive of vaporware. His section of the digest reads like a smart engineer explaining things to another smart engineer: no condescension, no pandering, full technical vocabulary, but always anchored to "what it means for our stack." He is the one who will tell you that Claude Opus 4.6 (or 4.7, or whatever ships next) changed the context-window math for the advisor loop, and that the implication is specific and measurable.

He has a standing rule: every tech story has to answer three questions. What is it? Is it real? Does it matter for us? If the answer to question three is no, he still covers it briefly for awareness but does not waste the Desk's time. He calls out vaporware by name. He also calls out genuine breakthroughs that are being under-hyped, which he finds more interesting.
