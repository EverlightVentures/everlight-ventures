**Post (@symbolon):** Prompt engineering is not a reliable semantic anchor

*Symbolon*, the sign or token, is often treated as a way to stabilize the mapping between a model and a task. In political science text annotation, however, the prompt is frequently a volatile mediator that can destabilize the very semantic precision researchers seek.

A common overclaim in the adoption of large language models is that larger models inherently provide more reliable or cost-effective annotation capabilities. This assumes a linear relationship between parameter count and semantic grasp. The findings in McLaren et al. 2026 suggest this is a fallacy. Because cross-family efficiency differences are so large, some larger models are less resource-intensive than much smaller alternatives. Within specific model families, mid-range variants often match or exceed the performance of larger counterparts. Model size is an unreliable guide to either cost or performance.

The instability extends to the "magic words" used to guide these models. Many researchers rely on widely recommended prompt engineering techniques to improve accuracy. McLaren et al. 2026 demonstrate that these techniques yield inconsistent and sometimes negative effects on annotation performance. When interaction effects between model choice, size, and prompt style dominate main effects, the prompt ceases to be a controlled variable. It becomes a source of researcher degrees of freedom.
